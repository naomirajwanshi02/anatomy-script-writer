### 03 Why it leaks: popcorn over a wall (5:02 to 6:59, 1,634 characters)

```txt
So why does a switch that's off leak at all? Let's zoom right into that gap under the gate and look at it the way an electron sees it.

Left side, the source, packed with electrons. Right side, the drain. In between, under the gate, there's a hill: an energy barrier the p-type body puts up. Switch off means a tall hill, so the electrons mostly stay home.

But electrons never sit still. Heat keeps kicking them around. Most get small kicks, a few get big ones. It's popcorn in a hot pan, bro.

Here's the pan. The wall is our barrier. Most kernels hop and drop back in. But every now and then, one jumps high enough to clear the wall... and it's out.

Now watch: I lower the wall just a little. Way more escape. Jump heights follow an exponential, so a slightly lower wall lets out MANY more kernels.

[thoughtful] Back to the transistor. The gate sets the height of the hill. It sits right above it like a capacitor plate, but the body holds on too, so the hill only follows part of what the gate does. That part comes back as n. Raise the gate, the hill drops, and exponentially more electrons spill into the drain. That's subthreshold current. Not a broken switch. Just heat and statistics.

This bar chart counts electrons at each energy. Only the thin tail above the hilltop makes it across, and I've exaggerated it so you can see it. Turn up the heat, and the tail gets fatter.
```

### 04 The one formula (6:59 to 9:01, 1,712 characters)

```txt
That hill picture is really all there is, and it boils down to one formula. Just one, and I'll translate every piece into plain words.

Leakage current equals I naught, times e to the power V G S minus V T H, over n times the thermal voltage, times one minus e to the minus V D S over the thermal voltage. V G S is gate to source, V T H the threshold, V D S drain to source.

I naught is mostly the size of the transistor: more width, more lanes for electrons. Twice as wide, twice the leak. It also folds in mobility and temperature.

This exponent is the big one: gate voltage minus threshold. Below the threshold it's negative, so the current falls off a cliff: ten times less for every sixty millivolts times n. With n of one point four, that's the eighty-three from the log plot.

The thermal voltage, k T over q, is temperature in disguise: about twenty six millivolts at room temperature. Heat the chip and it grows. Hold that thought.

Then n: how firmly the gate grips the channel. It's a tug of war: the gate pulls the channel one way, the body pulls it back, so the channel only follows part of what the gate does. A perfect grip is n equals one. Real planar transistors sit around one point four.

Last bit, the drain term. Once the drain is more than about a hundred millivolts up, it's basically one. And in a real circuit the off transistor has the full supply across it, so this term never saves you.

Real numbers: zero volts on the gate, a threshold of three hundred fifty millivolts, n of one point four. The exponent comes out about minus nine point seven, and e to that is one in sixteen thousand. Times I naught, that's about a hundred picoamps. Tiny on its own. Huge times fifteen billion.
```

### 05 The 60 millivolt wall (9:01 to 10:26, 1,253 characters)

```txt
Engineers have a name for that slope: the subthreshold swing, S. It's how many millivolts you have to move the gate to change the current ten times.

With a perfect gate, at room temperature, that's about sixty millivolts. And here's the brutal part: at room temperature, no normal transistor can beat that. Not with better design, not with more money.

[thoughtful] Why sixty? Every twenty-six millivolts you lower the hill, the current grows by e, about two point seven. Ten times needs two point three of those steps: sixty millivolts. That's physics, not engineering: the Boltzmann tyranny. Only cooling the chip way down lowers it.

Real transistors sit above it. Old-school planar ones, around eighty-five. FinFETs, sixty-five to seventy. Gate-all-around, a bit closer still.

Why fuss over a few millivolts? Say the threshold is three hundred fifty millivolts and the swing is eighty-three. From threshold down to zero, you only get about four decades of off, four steps of ten. A sharper slope squeezes more off out of the same threshold.

That's why labs chase steep-slope devices, like tunnel FETs, where electrons tunnel through the barrier instead of hopping over it, to beat sixty. They're not in your phone yet. For now, sixty is the wall.
```

### 07 Heat: leakage's best friend (12:23 to 13:59, 1,413 characters)

```txt
Remember the thermal voltage, the heat hiding in the formula? Let's actually heat the chip up. This is where leakage gets nasty.

Heat hits leakage twice. The thermal voltage grows, so the slope gets lazier. And the threshold itself drops, one to two millivolts per degree: hot silicon makes more free charge of its own, so the gate needs less push to form that layer. Both push the off current up.

At eighty-five degrees, which a phone chip hits easily under load, this transistor's off current is about twenty-five times higher than at room temperature.

Rule of thumb from the industry: leakage roughly doubles every ten to fifteen degrees. The exact number changes from process to process, but the shape never does. Exponential.

And now the scary part: a loop. Leakage makes heat, heat makes more leakage. With good cooling, the loop settles at a steady temperature.

With poor cooling, it never settles. The temperature keeps climbing until the chip has to throttle itself. That's thermal runaway.

[warmly] You've felt this. A phone left in a hot car drains faster. A laptop with a blocked fan throttles. That's why leakage specs are quoted hot, at eighty-five or one twenty-five degrees.

Pro tip: at really low supply voltages, a chip can get faster when it's hot, because the falling threshold beats the slower electrons. That's temperature inversion, and why sign-off checks both hot and cold corners.
```

### 08 Short channels and DIBL (13:59 to 15:52, 1,635 characters)

```txt
Shrinking didn't just drag the threshold down. It also squeezed the source and drain closer and closer together, and that opened up a whole new leak: short channels.

Back to the hill. In a long transistor, only the gate decides how tall the hill is. The drain is too far away to mess with it.

Now shrink the channel. The drain gets so close that its voltage reaches under the gate and drags the hill down from the side. That's called threshold roll-off: the shorter the channel, the lower the threshold, even with almost no drain voltage.

Crank up the drain voltage, and the hill drops even more. That's called D I B L, drain-induced barrier lowering. The drain is doing the gate's job, and nobody asked it to.

We measure it in millivolts of threshold lost per volt on the drain. This planar one loses about eighty: at three quarters of a volt, that's sixty millivolts gone, about five times the leak. Add the roll-off, and this short transistor leaks about seventy times more than a long one. And the off transistor in a gate has the full supply across it.

[thoughtful] Two rooms, one wall. Thick wall, you never hear the neighbours. Thin wall, their music comes right through. Short channel, thin wall.

The fix: give the gate more grip. Wrap it around the channel so the drain has less say. Planar has the gate on one side; a FinFET wraps three. Intel put FinFETs into mass production at twenty-two nanometres around twenty twelve.

Gate-all-around nanosheets wrap all four sides. Samsung started making them at three nanometres in twenty twenty-two, and the other big foundries followed. Look at the hill now: it barely moves.
```

### 09 Body bias and the stack trick (15:52 to 17:47, 1,611 characters)

```txt
So the gate isn't the only thing that sets the hill. Two more knobs do too, and chip designers use both. Knob one: the body.

The transistor sits on a body of silicon underneath, and the body acts like a weak second gate. That's called the body effect. Pull the body below the source, that's reverse body bias, and the threshold goes up: less leakage. Push it the other way, forward body bias, and the threshold drops: faster, but leakier.

In older bulk chips this was a solid standby trick. In FinFETs, the body barely touches the thin fin, so it hardly works. In F D S O I chips, a thin film on buried glass, the body acts like a real back gate, so designers tune it on the fly. Push too hard, though, and junction leaks take over.

Knob two is almost free: stacking. A NAND gate has two P-moss side by side up top and two N-moss in a chain down to ground. With both inputs low, that chain has two off transistors in series. You'd guess half the leak, but it's usually five to ten times less.

Here's why. The tiny leak charges the middle point to a few tens of millivolts. So the top transistor's gate ends up below its source, it gets extra body effect, and its drain sees less voltage, so less D I B L. Three wins.

That's why a NAND gate's leak depends on its inputs. Both inputs zero: two off N-moss stacked, lowest leak. One input high: only one transistor blocking. Both high: two off P-moss side by side, the worst.

Good designs use this. Before a block goes to sleep, they park its inputs in the lowest-leak pattern. Some even split a transistor into two stacked ones wherever speed doesn't matter.
```

### 10 Why the average lies (17:47 to 18:47, 890 characters)

```txt
So far we've talked about one transistor at a time. But a chip has billions, and here's what catches even experienced people: no two come out identical. A few atoms here or there, and the threshold shifts by tens of millivolts.

Leakage is exponential in the threshold, so that spread isn't fair. The few low-threshold transistors leak way more than the rest. Here, the leakiest five percent carry about a third of all the leakage.

So a real chip leaks more than a chip built from average transistors: the few leaky ones drag the average about one point eight times above the typical one. Estimate with typical numbers, and you'll always come in low.

[warmly] Same thing with whole chips. The ones that come out fast usually leak more, because their thresholds landed low. That's part of why one processor gets sold in different speed and power grades. Gamers call it the silicon lottery.
```

### 12 The leakage toolbox (19:54 to 21:21, 1,262 characters)

```txt
Cutting the power is one tool. Here's the whole toolbox chip designers fight leakage with, one tool at a time.

Tool one: different threshold flavours. Fast, leaky, low-threshold transistors only on the few paths that set the clock speed; slow, tight, high-threshold ones everywhere else. Most of a chip doesn't need to be fast, so leakage drops several times.

Tool two: power gating. A big, tight, high-threshold switch called a sleep transistor sits between the supply and the block. Turn it off, and the whole block stops leaking. The catch: waking up takes time and a rush of current, and special flip-flops have to remember the state.

Think of your house. Turning off the lights is clock gating: stop the clock, nothing switches, no switching power. Flipping the breaker for the whole room is power gating: now even the leaks stop.

Tool three: reverse body bias in standby, where the process allows it. Tool four: drop the supply in standby to just enough to keep memory alive. Both only help the blocks that are still awake.

And tool five: better transistors. FinFETs and gate-all-around give the gate more grip: a steeper slope and less D I B L. The tools overlap, so you can't just multiply them, but together leakage drops about eight hundred times.
```

### 13 Plot twist (21:21 to 22:29, 956 characters)

```txt
[curious] After all that fighting, here's the plot twist: some chips run below the threshold on purpose.

If you care about battery life and not speed, you can drop the supply below the threshold. The chip gets slow, but each operation costs way less energy. And it still works: the current isn't zero, it's that exponential tail. Go too low, though, and every operation takes so long that leakage eats the savings. The sweet spot is called the minimum energy point, and it sits near or below the threshold.

Tiny chips in smartwatches, hearing aids and sensors live right here. Analog designers love this region too: the most gain per microamp. The same physics that's a bug in your CPU is a feature somewhere else.
```

- `What is subthreshold leakage (explainer, full).html`: the whole video with the hand cursor. Space plays, arrows seek, `[` `]` jump chapters, S shows subtitles.

## Production notes

- **Build:** `npm run build:subthreshold-longform` (writes every page in this folder and this script).
- **Preview:** open the full page in Chrome and press Space. `?t=300` starts at 5:00, `?subs` shows the spoken line, `?autoplay` starts at once.
- **Render one .mov per chapter** (ProRes, next to each page; 1920 x 1080, 60 fps; `--scale 2` for 4K):
```bash
node builds/capture.mjs "shorts/longform/What is subthreshold leakage (17 min)/chapters" --size 1920x1080 --scale 1 --fps 60
```

Chapter pages butt-join seamlessly in Premiere: each one is an exact slice of the same timeline. The full page renders the same way in one go.
- **Retime after recording:** generate one take per chapter, read each beat's real start time (Premiere markers or a transcript), write them into `src/explainers/subthreshold-longform/timing.ts` (`TIMING`, by beat id) and rebuild. Every cursor move stays on its word; a uniformly faster or slower chapter only needs `CH_RATE`.
- **Numbers:** every value on screen and in the voice comes from `physics.ts` (one device model: Vth 0.35 V at 0.75 V drain, n = 1.4, Ioff 100 pA, Ion 49 µA at 27 °C). The generations chart is an illustrative trend.
