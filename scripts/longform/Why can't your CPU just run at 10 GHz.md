### 01 How long is one tick? (0:59 to 2:36, 1397 characters)

```txt
As we know, everything a CPU does comes down to logic gates, and every gate is built from transistors. And it all marches to a clock: one step per tick. 1 GHz means a billion ticks per second, so each tick lasts one nanosecond.

So at ten gigahertz, how long is one tick? One divided by 10 GHz is 100 picoseconds. Light moves at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. In 100 picoseconds, it covers about 3 centimetres. Let's say two neighbouring gates sit 10 micrometres apart. Light crosses that in about 0.03 picoseconds. So what does hold a gate back?

Imagine gate A feeding gate B. Gate B's input behaves like a tiny capacitor, two plates that store charge, a small bucket for electrons. Gate A's switched-on transistor acts roughly like a resistor, a narrow pipe into it. The output is the voltage on that bucket, and it fills along a curve, not in a jump. The delay is counted until it's half full: about 0.69 times R times C, the resistance times the capacitance. Let's say R is 10 kilohms and C is 1 femtofarad, a thousandth of a picofarad. That gives about 7 picoseconds.

So inside a gate, the bottleneck isn't the speed of light, it's filling the bucket. A stronger transistor or a smaller load makes the gate faster. This small wait is called the gate delay, or propagation delay. Rounding up, let's say one gate delay is 10 picoseconds. Then our 100 picosecond tick holds just ten of them.
```

### 02 What has to fit inside one tick? (2:36 to 4:12, 1368 characters)

```txt
Now you might be wondering: if one gate is that quick, why not tick once per gate? Take a 4-bit adder for example, adding 7 plus 1. It's four full adders in a row, each adding two bits plus a carry, the 1 spilling over from the column before. In binary, 0111 plus 0001 should give 1000, which is 8. The carry has to ripple through every adder before the answer settles. Let's say each adder passes its carry on in 2 gate delays. That's 8 in total.

Before and after the adder sit flip-flops, little one-bit memories that copy their input at the tick and hold it until the next. So what if we tick every couple of gate delays?

After 2 gate delays, only the first adder has finished. The output flip-flop grabs 0110, which is 6, not 8. The CPU just stored a wrong answer, and it can't tell.

So the tick has to wait for the slowest path from one flip-flop to the next. Plus the flip-flops' own time: a delay to show a new value after the tick, and a moment the input must sit still before it. These are the clock-to-Q delay, Q being the flip-flop's output, and the setup time. And the slowest path is called the critical path. Let's say each flip-flop time is one gate delay. Then the tick is 1 plus 8 plus 1 gate delays, which is 100 picoseconds. So this little adder tops out at 10 GHz. Each transistor is quick; it's the chain of gates between two flip-flops that is long.
```

### 03 Cut the path into pieces (4:12 to 6:16, 1779 characters)

```txt
Okay, but can't we just cut the path shorter? Imagine one instruction needs 40 gate delays of logic, plus 2 of flip-flop overhead, so 42 per tick. Now drop in flip-flops after every 10 gate delays. Each piece needs 10 plus 2, and the clock runs 42 divided by 12, so 3.5 times faster.

Once every piece is busy, one instruction finishes every tick. This is a pipeline, and each piece is a stage. Notice that one instruction isn't faster: its latency, start to finish, is 4 pieces of 12, 48 gate delays. Only throughput, the finishing rate, goes up, and an instruction waiting on another's result can stall it.

So why not give every gate its own stage? Here's the catch: every stage still pays the full flip-flop overhead. Each tick becomes 1 plus 2 gate delays: two thirds of it overhead, so each extra cut buys less and less.

And there's a second problem. A branch is an instruction that decides what runs next, an "if" in your code. The CPU only learns which way it goes deep in the pipeline, so rather than wait, it guesses. A wrong guess throws away everything behind the branch, and the deeper the pipeline, the more it wastes. This is the branch misprediction penalty.

So there's a sweet spot. A 2002 study measured stages in FO4 delays. That's fan-out of four: an inverter, the simplest gate, driving four copies of itself, roughly one ordinary gate delay. The study found integer programs, the everyday whole-number kind, ran best at about 7.8 per stage: 6 of useful logic and 1.8 of overhead.

The first Pentium 4 bet on depth: 20 pipeline stages that a wrong branch guess must refill, double the Pentium III's 10. The Prescott core of 2004 stretched that to 31. And the 3.8 GHz Pentium 4 570 had a thermal design power, the heat its cooler is built to carry away, of 115 watts.
```

### 04 What does one tick cost? (6:16 to 8:01, 1511 characters)

```txt
Why would ticking faster make a chip hotter? Back to our bucket. Every time gate A fills it, the supply hands over an energy of C times V squared, where V is the supply voltage. Half is stored in the bucket, and half turns into heat in the transistor. When the bucket empties to ground, the circuit's zero point, the stored half becomes heat too.

Now imagine a chip with 1 billion of these buckets, each 0.2 femtofarads, at 1 volt. One in ten fills each tick, at 5 GHz. Multiply it out: that's 100 watts.

Notice that at the same voltage each tick costs the same energy, but power is energy per second, so it grows with the clock frequency. At 10 GHz, our 100 watts becomes 200.

Here's where things get worse. A faster tick needs faster gates, and the knob for that is voltage: more voltage means more charge, but a transistor isn't a plain resistor, so its current grows even faster, and the bucket fills sooner. Let's say 20 percent more frequency needs about 20 percent more voltage. Power then grows by 1.2, times 1.2 squared, so about 1.73 times. Roughly, when voltage rises with frequency, power grows with the cube of frequency. So doubling our chip from 5 to 10 GHz wouldn't cost 200 watts, but about 800. But that cube rule only fits small steps: so much voltage would wreck the transistors first.

This is dynamic power: alpha, times C, times V squared, times f. Alpha, the activity factor, is the share of the chip switching each tick. So the deeper pipeline bought more ticks, and every tick costs energy.
```

### 05 How clocks climbed for 30 years (8:01 to 9:02, 871 characters)

```txt
So how did clocks climb for decades without melting everything? Imagine we shrink every length and voltage of a transistor to 0.7, even the voltage where it switches on. ### 06 The knob that got stuck (9:02 to 10:40, 1425 characters)
```

```txt
But that trick needs the voltage to drop with every shrink. So why didn't it keep dropping? A transistor only switches on once the voltage on its control input climbs above a certain point. Let's say the supply is 1 volt and that switch point is 0.3 volts, leaving 0.7 volts of push. Drop the supply to 0.7 volts but keep that switch point, and only 0.4 volts of push is left, so gates get slower. So lower the switch point too?

But an off transistor is never fully off; a tiny current still leaks through it. And that leak grows exponentially as the switch point drops. For example, lower the switch point by 0.1 volts, and every off transistor might leak ten times more. Why not a sharper switch? Heat makes electrons jiggle, so a few always hop the barrier, the energy hill, that holds them back. The switch point is called the threshold voltage, and the off current is leakage, which burns static power even when nothing switches.

By the mid-2000s, leakage made it hard to lower the threshold voltage, and so the supply voltage. Say we rerun our shrink with the voltage stuck at 1. Power per transistor is 0.7 times 1 times 1.43, about 1. The area is still 0.49, so power density roughly doubles every generation.

In May 2004, Intel cancelled Tejas, the planned follow-on to the Pentium 4, and moved to dual-core designs, two processors on one chip. So notice: the knob that got stuck wasn't switching speed. It was voltage.
```

### 07 Why not just a bigger cooler? (10:40 to 11:55, 1083 characters)

```txt
Okay, but can't we just bolt on a bigger cooler? Heat leaves the silicon through a chain: the die, the bare slice of silicon, then the lid, paste, cooler and air. Each link resists heat, and the temperature rise is the power times that resistance. Let's say the chain adds up to 0.3 degrees Celsius per watt. At 250 watts, the silicon sits 75 degrees above a 25 degree room: 100 degrees Celsius.

A bigger cooler only shrinks its own link. The heat still has to squeeze out of a small sliver of silicon, and the busiest spots run hotter than average. Let me make it even worse: hotter silicon leaks more, which makes more heat. How hard each link resists heat is called its thermal resistance, and this whole squeeze is the heat-density wall, often called the power wall.

So chips set a hard temperature limit and slow down when they reach it. This is called thermal throttling. The 14900KS is rated for a maximum turbo power of 253 watts, and a maximum junction temperature, the hottest the silicon itself may get, of 100 degrees Celsius. So what's that more ordinary thing? It's heat.
```

### 08 Two slow cores beat one fast one (11:55 to 13:04, 960 characters)

```txt
So what do chip makers do instead? A core is one complete processor, with its own pipeline. Imagine core A runs at frequency 1 and voltage 1, so its power is 1. Now consider two cores at 0.8 frequency and 0.8 voltage. Each uses 0.8 times 0.8 squared, about 0.51. Together, about 1.02, the same dynamic power. But they do 1.6 times the work. Feels a bit like cheating, right? Well, there's a catch.

Only the part of the work that can be split runs on both cores. Let's say half of a program must run step by step. Even with two full-speed cores, that half still takes 0.5, and the other half takes 0.25. So the program takes 0.75 of the time: about 1.33 times faster, not 2. At our 0.8 speed, that 0.75 stretches to about 0.94: only about 7 percent faster than core A. This limit is known as Amdahl's law.

And the 14900KS has 24 cores: 8 fast ones and 16 small efficient ones. The fast ones take the step-by-step part; on work that splits, the small ones join in.
```

### 09 What "max turbo 6.2 GHz" really means (13:04 to 14:36, 1289 characters)

```txt
So, going back to the spec sheet: why is that top number only a maximum? The 14900KS's base clock on its performance cores is 3.2 GHz, and 6.2 GHz is the maximum. The chip watches itself: with a core or two busy and the silicon cool, it raises their voltage and frequency, then steps down as heat builds.

This is called a boost clock. It's not your CPU's everyday speed; it's a top speed a core or two may briefly reach.

So could one core just run at ten gigahertz? Well, let's find out. At 6.2 GHz, a tick lasts about 161 picoseconds; at 10 GHz, only 100. So each stage gets fewer gates, or a deeper pipeline adds overhead and branch losses. Even if the voltage could climb that far, and it can't, the cube rule gives about 4.2 times the power. Let's say one core draws 40 watts at 6.2 GHz. At 10 GHz, that would be about 170 watts from a single core, on a tiny patch of silicon.

So, are the transistors too slow? Not really: more voltage makes them faster, but costs heat. Is it the speed of light? No, but long wires add their own delay: each is a thin pipe with its own bucket to fill. Signals that cross the whole chip get boosters along the way, or several ticks. It's the critical path, dynamic power, and the heat-density wall. The clock didn't hit a speed limit; it hit a heat limit.
```

### 10 Take the heat away (14:36 to 15:35, 855 characters)

```txt
Before ending this video, I want to talk about the opposite extreme. What if we take the heat away completely? An overclocker is someone who pushes a chip past its rated speed. Now, cold does two things: it carries heat away, so the chip can take more voltage, and it makes transistors leak less and switch faster. So why stop short? Power per transistor is 0.7 times 1 times 1.43, about 1. The area is still 0.49, so power density roughly doubles every generation. | The shrink panel returns with the voltage locked at 1 V; power density doubles each generation. | Nothing. |
```
| <b>10:26.0-10:40.5</b> (6.4) In May 2004, Intel cancelled Tejas, the planned follow-on to the Pentium 4, and moved to dual-core designs, two processors on one chip. So notice: the knob that got stuck wasn't switching speed. It was voltage. | History plot at 2004: Tejas cancelled; a dual-core tag; the line flattens to today. | Nothing. |
| <b>10:40 · Chapter 7: Why not just a bigger cooler?</b> |  |  |
| <b>10:41.5-11:08.9</b> (7.1) Okay, but can't we just bolt on a bigger cooler? Heat leaves the silicon through a chain: the die, the bare slice of silicon, then the lid, paste, cooler and air. Each link resists heat, and the temperature rise is the power times that resistance. Let's say the chain adds up to 0.3 degrees Celsius per watt. At 250 watts, the silicon sits 75 degrees above a 25 degree room: 100 degrees Celsius. | A cut-away stack of die, lid, paste, cooler and air with heat arrows and resistance labels. | Nothing. |
| <b>11:08.9-11:34.0</b> (7.2) A bigger cooler only shrinks its own link. The heat still has to squeeze out of a small sliver of silicon, and the busiest spots run hotter than average. Let me make it even worse: hotter silicon leaks more, which makes more heat. How hard each link resists heat is called its thermal resistance, and this whole squeeze is the heat-density wall, often called the power wall. | The cooler layer grows but only its own label shrinks; hot spots glow on the die. | Nothing. |
| <b>11:34.0-11:55.3</b> (7.3) So chips set a hard temperature limit and slow down when they reach it. This is called thermal throttling. The 14900KS is rated for a maximum turbo power of 253 watts, and a maximum junction temperature, the hottest the silicon itself may get, of 100 degrees Celsius. So what's that more ordinary thing? It's heat. | The i9-14900KS card returns; a thermometer creeps to the red line and the clock dial steps down; 253 W and 100 °C light. | Nothing. |
| <b>11:55 · Chapter 8: Two slow cores beat one fast one</b> |  |  |
| <b>11:56.3-12:24.6</b> (8.1) So what do chip makers do instead? A core is one complete processor, with its own pipeline. Imagine core A runs at frequency 1 and voltage 1, so its power is 1. Now consider two cores at 0.8 frequency and 0.8 voltage. Each uses 0.8 times 0.8 squared, about 0.51. Together, about 1.02, the same dynamic power. But they do 1.6 times the work. Feels a bit like cheating, right? Well, there's a catch. | One large hot core; then two smaller, dimmer cores with power and work bars. | Nothing. |
| <b>12:24.6-12:53.1</b> (8.2) Only the part of the work that can be split runs on both cores. Let's say half of a program must run step by step. Even with two full-speed cores, that half still takes 0.5, and the other half takes 0.25. So the program takes 0.75 of the time: about 1.33 times faster, not 2. At our 0.8 speed, that 0.75 stretches to about 0.94: only about 7 percent faster than core A. This limit is known as Amdahl's law. | Partner program bar: grey serial half and orange parallel half; the parallel half shortens to 0.75, then both stretch to 0.94. | Nothing. |
| <b>12:53.1-13:04.3</b> (8.3) And the 14900KS has 24 cores: 8 fast ones and 16 small efficient ones. The fast ones take the step-by-step part; on work that splits, the small ones join in. | The i9 card: the cores row lights and a core map shows 8 large and 16 small cores. | Nothing. |
| <b>13:04 · Chapter 9: What "max turbo 6.2 GHz" really means</b> |  |  |
| <b>13:05.3-13:26.3</b> (9.1) So, going back to the spec sheet: why is that top number only a maximum? The 14900KS's base clock on its performance cores is 3.2 GHz, and 6.2 GHz is the maximum. The chip watches itself: with a core or two busy and the silicon cool, it raises their voltage and frequency, then steps down as heat builds. | A dial with base 3.2 and max 6.2 marks; one core loads and the needle climbs, then steps back as the temperature rises. | Nothing. |
| <b>13:26.3-13:34.8</b> (9.2) This is called a boost clock. It's not your CPU's everyday speed; it's a top speed a core or two may briefly reach. | The label boost clock stamps onto the dial. | Nothing. |
| <b>13:34.8-14:07.5</b> (9.3) So could one core just run at ten gigahertz? Well, let's find out. At 6.2 GHz, a tick lasts about 161 picoseconds; at 10 GHz, only 100. So each stage gets fewer gates, or a deeper pipeline adds overhead and branch losses. Even if the voltage could climb that far, and it can't, the cube rule gives about 4.2 times the power. Let's say one core draws 40 watts at 6.2 GHz. At 10 GHz, that would be about 170 watts from a single core, on a tiny patch of silicon. | Two meters: tick length and one core's power; the clock slider goes to 10 GHz; a red dashed outline marks the tiny patch. | Nothing. |
| <b>14:07.5-14:36.6</b> (9.4) So, are the transistors too slow? Not really: more voltage makes them faster, but costs heat. Is it the speed of light? No, but long wires add their own delay: each is a thin pipe with its own bucket to fill. Signals that cross the whole chip get boosters along the way, or several ticks. It's the critical path, dynamic power, and the heat-density wall. The clock didn't hit a speed limit; it hit a heat limit. | The three question cards return with their answers: a voltage knob tied to a flame, a wire that fills like a bucket, a box that opens to a flame. | Nothing. |
| <b>14:36 · Chapter 10: Take the heat away</b> |  |  |
| <b>14:37.6-15:00.6</b> (10.1) Before ending this video, I want to talk about the opposite extreme. What if we take the heat away completely? An overclocker is someone who pushes a chip past its rated speed. In a record reported in May 2026, one of them cooled an Intel Core i9-14900KF with liquid helium, about minus 269 degrees Celsius, and ran one core at 9,206 MHz. | A chip in a frosted copper pot with vapour; the frequency counter spins up to 9,206 MHz. | Nothing. |
| <b>15:00.6-15:35.1</b> (10.2) Now, cold does two things: it carries heat away, so the chip can take more voltage, and it makes transistors leak less and switch faster. So why stop short? Past a certain voltage, transistors get damaged, so with voltage capped, at some clock the critical path won't fit inside a tick. And in a normal personal computer, heat at that clock just can't be removed fast enough. So, remember that myth from the start? Clocks didn't stop because transistors couldn't switch faster, but because the heat had nowhere to go. | A voltage bar hits a red dashed line; the frost fades and an ordinary cooler appears, glowing red. | Nothing. |
| <b>15:35 · Chapter 11: Wrap</b> |  |  |
| <b>15:36.1-16:08.1</b> (11.1) And that's it for now. I simplified the tick, though. We skipped clock skew, the tick reaching parts of the chip at different times. We skipped hold time, how long an input must stay put after the tick. A 2011 study projected that at the 8-nanometre generation, more than half of a fixed-size chip would have to stay powered off. That's called dark silicon. So if we can't power all those transistors at once, why keep adding more? That's where we'll pick up next time. | A large grid of transistors; whole patches go dark. | Nothing. |
